PW-1x Assembly Guide

Every part is bought. This is the order to put them together, what each step should look and feel like, and where to stop and check. Written for a first server build: nothing is assumed, every term is explained the first time it appears, and each risky step has a drawing. The graphics card stays in the desktop; PW-1x runs on its processor alone until a card is bought.

Part 2 · The build

Twelve steps, in this order

Each step says what you are doing, what it should look or feel like when it is right, and the one condition where you stop instead of pushing on. Steps 1 to 5 are the board on the bench with no cable between it and the power supply. Step 7 is the first time the supply's switch is turned on. Budget an unhurried two to three hours for steps 0 to 9, then the night for step 10.

  1. Before opening anything

    Open bench means the board lies flat on its box with the parts mounted on it and the power supply beside it, no case. Workshop servers run like this for years. What it costs you is that nothing pushes air across the board except the cooler's own fans; step 9 reads the temperatures that tell you whether that matters.

    Static — the board’s own instructions, which replace what this guide said before. The printed Quick Installation Guide lists five precautions, and two of them set the procedure: unplug the power cord from the wall socket before touching any components, and never place the server board directly on the carpet or the like; use a grounded wrist strap or touch a safety grounded object before you handle the components. So the AC cord stays out of the wall until step 7. Earlier revisions of this guide had you plug it in at step 0 to ground the supply’s shell; the board’s manual says otherwise, and it wins.

    What to do instead, best first: clip the wrist strap to the supply’s bare steel shell and wear it for the whole build. With the cord out, that shell is not earthed — but earthing is not the part that matters. The strap holds you, the supply and the board at the same potential, and a discharge needs a difference. Without a strap, do what the manual’s second precaution says: touch a safety grounded object — a radiator, the metal shell of a plugged-in appliance, the faceplate screw of a grounded outlet — before you pick up any part, and again every time you have walked away and come back. Leave every part in its bag until the step that uses it.

    Find these on the real board with the Quick Installation Guide open at its Motherboard Layout panel — section 2, the big drawing with 44 numbered callouts. Every name below is the one printed on that drawing and on the board itself:

    • The processor socket: a large square with a hinged metal frame and three screws, silkscreened LGA4094 Socket SP3. The board is 24.4 × 30.5 cm (9.6 × 12 in) — full ATX.
    • The eight memory slots, DDR4_A1 to DDR4_H1, in two banks of four with the socket between them: A1–D1 on the side away from the power connectors, E1–H1 on the same side as them.
    • Both M.2 slots — M2_1 and M2_2, side by side below the socket. They are not interchangeable here; step 3 says which one to use and why.
    • ATXPWR1, the wide 24-pin power connector along one edge.
    • ATX12V1, an 8-pin power connector, and ATX12V2, a 4-pin one. Both feed the processor. Both get plugged in.
    • PANEL1, the system panel header — the block of small pins a case’s power button would plug into — with AUX_PANEL1 beside it and SPEAKER1 nearby. Two pins of PANEL1 are the power switch; the full User’s Manual says which.
    • The fan headers — and there is no processor fan header. This board has seven, FAN1 through FAN7, and the layout calls every one of them a System Fan Connector. FAN1, FAN2 and FAN3 are the group nearest the socket; FAN4–FAN7 sit further out. The cooler goes on FAN1.
    • Dr. Debug, a two-digit display near TPM1. It shows a code for whatever stage the board is stuck at, which is worth more than any amount of guessing at step 7.
    • The two selection jumpers PE8_SEL and PE16_SEL, and the CLRMOS1 clear-CMOS pad, in the header row along the bottom edge. Do not move the jumpers yet; step 3 explains what they switch.
    • On the rear panel, in the order the quick guide’s I/O Panel drawing gives: a small UID1 switch, VGA1, the COM1 serial port, two USB 3.2 Gen1 ports, the management network port IPMI_LAN1, then the two 10GbE ports LAN1 and LAN2, then a USB-C port. The small rear button is UID1, an identification light, not a power button. Pressing it does nothing you want and nothing you cannot undo.
    IPMI / BMCthe board has a small computer of its own, the baseboard management controller, that runs whenever the power supply is switched on, even with the machine "off". It serves a web page on the management port where you can power the machine on, watch the screen and read every temperature sensor. It is also what drives the VGA port.
    Looks right when

    You can point to every item on the list above on the real board, with the quick guide closed.

    Stop if

    A label on the sheet does not match what is silkscreened on the board — the sheet covers both the ROMED8-2T and the ROMED8-NL, and boards have revisions. Trust the print on the board.

  2. Processor into the socket

    Ground yourself first: wrist strap on the supply’s shell, or touch a grounded metal object. Again every time you sit back down.

    This is the one step where force in the wrong place does permanent damage, and it is also simple if you follow the order. The socket’s metal frame is held by three captive T20 Torx screws — they loosen but do not come out — and the board’s own Quick Installation Guide (section 5) labels them A, B, C. Its instruction is the same in both directions: unscrew A → B → C, and fasten A → B → C.

    On the order, because two sources disagree

    AMD’s own SP3 documentation numbers these screws 1, 2, 3 and says to tighten 1→2→3 but loosen in reverse, 3→2→1. ASRock’s sheet for this board says A→B→C both ways. Revisions of this guide before 14 Sep 2026 followed AMD and told you to open 3-2-1.

    The two agree on the direction that matters. Fastening is what seats the processor evenly, and both say ascending — first screw, second, third. Opening only releases spring tension; nothing is being seated, so the order costs much less. Read what is actually stamped on your frame, and follow the sheet that came in the box: ascending both ways. If you would rather open in reverse because AMD says so, that is defensible too and breaks nothing.

    • Loosen the first screw, then the second, then the third. Lift the outer force frame up on its hinge — ASRock’s sheet calls this "the first retention cover".
    • Under it is a thinner rail frame, ASRock’s "second bracket". Lift it up on its hinge too.
    • Take out the internal plastic cover. A protective plastic insert sits in the rail frame from the factory and has to come out before the processor goes in — ASRock’s step 4, and something earlier revisions of this guide never mentioned. Keep it: it goes back in if the board is ever stored or returned. Now the socket’s gold contacts are exposed. Do not touch them and do not let anything fall on them.
    • Take the processor, still in its plastic carrier. There is a small triangle on one corner of the carrier and a matching triangle on the rail frame. Match them.
    • Slide the carrier into the rail frame's guides from the top until it clicks. It should sit flat in the rail frame with no effort; you are sliding plastic into plastic, not pushing the processor into anything.
    • Lower the rail frame. Lower the force frame over it.
    • Tighten the first screw, then the second, then the third — ascending, which both sources agree on. Each screw turns freely, then stops. That stop is the end of travel: the socket is designed so that seated is seated. If you have a torque driver, 1.58 N·m (AMD’s figure; ASRock’s sheet gives no torque); if not, snug and stop.
    carrier framethe plastic surround the processor ships in. On this socket the processor is never handled bare; the carrier is what you hold, and it stays on the chip inside the socket. ASRock’s sheet puts it twice, in bold and in a warning triangle: install the carrier frame with the CPU, don’t separate them. It also recommends AMD’s CPU installation tool — the plastic handle some EPYC chips ship with — if you have one; the carrier alone is the normal way.
    Feels right when

    The carrier slid in with no resistance and clicked; all three screws turned freely and stopped at the same firmness.

    Stop if

    A screw resists before it stops, or the frame will not sit flat. Open it again in the same order and check that the plastic cover came out and the carrier is fully in its guides. Never push harder.

  3. Memory, all eight sticks

    Ground yourself first: wrist strap on the supply’s shell, or touch a grounded metal object. Again every time you sit back down.

    Every slot gets a stick, so there is no choice about which slots to use. The board is one DIMM per channel: eight channels, eight slots, and the bandwidth this whole machine is built around only exists with all eight filled.

    • Unlock the slot by pressing its clip outward. These slots have one clip, not two — the board’s own sheet (section 7) shows a single clip at one end and a fixed lip at the other. Earlier revisions of this guide said both ends latch; they do not.
    • Look at the gold edge of the stick: the notch is off-centre. Turn the stick until the notch lines up with the ridge in the slot. It only fits one way.
    • Set the stick in the slot, then press straight down on both ends at once, evenly. The clip usually swings up and clicks as it seats — but ASRock makes "lock the clip" a separate third step, so treat the click as a bonus and close the clip deliberately with a fingertip. Seat the fixed end first and the clip end follows.
    • Repeat for all eight. Then run a finger along every clip: each one fully closed and flush, every stick at the same height.
    ECC RDIMMserver memory. ECC means it detects and corrects single-bit errors instead of silently corrupting data; registered (the R) means a buffer chip on each stick lets the board run eight of them at full speed. It is not interchangeable with desktop memory.
    Feels right when

    Each stick took firm, even pressure, sits at the same height as its neighbours, and its clip is locked flush.

    Stop if

    A stick needs real force. It is turned the wrong way. Take it out and match the notch.

  4. The SSD into the second M.2 slot

    Ground yourself first: wrist strap on the supply’s shell, or touch a grounded metal object. Again every time you sit back down.

    The Kingston stick is 80mm long, size 2280. The slot has nut positions at several lengths and the board’s own sheet (section 10) names them. For M2_2 they run A 3cm (2230), B 4.2cm (2242), C 6cm (2260), D 8cm (2280), E 11cm (22110). Your drive uses position D. M2_1 has the same positions minus E, which is why it stops at 2280.

    Use M2_2, not M2_1

    The board has two M.2 sockets and they are not equivalent. The Quick Installation Guide’s jumper table (section 4) shows that M2_1 shares its lanes with the PCIE2 slot, the second Mini-SAS connector and the two OCuLink ports, and which of them are live is set by the PE8_SEL and PE16_SEL jumpers. In two of the four documented jumper positions — both of the ones where PE16_SEL is on pins 1-2 — M2_1 is switched off entirely. A drive in it would simply not exist: no error, no light, just nothing in the BIOS.

    M2_2 does not appear in that table at all, so no jumper can take it away, and it accepts every length up to 22110. The 2280 Kingston fits it. Put the drive there and the trap never comes up. If you ever do want M2_1, PE16_SEL has to be on pins 2-3, and you give up PCIE2 running at full x16 to get it.

    Found on 14 Sep 2026 by reading the board’s own quick guide. Nothing before this had it, including this guide.

    • Find M2_2 — of the two side-by-side M.2 slots, it is the one whose silkscreen reads M2_2. Check the print; do not go by which is on the left.
    • Move the standoff to position D, the 8cm nut location. Peel the yellow protective film off the nut first — it ships with one on, ASRock says so in step 3, and nothing else in this build has a film to remove. Then hand-tighten the standoff; no driver.
    • Hold the stick at about 20 degrees and push its gold edge into the slot until it stops. It will spring up at an angle; that is normal.
    • Press it flat against the standoff and fix it with the small screw. Snug, not tight — ASRock’s step 5 says in as many words not to overtighten. The screw only stops the stick springing back up.
    M.2 / NVMea solid-state drive that plugs straight into the board and talks over PCI Express, the same bus a graphics card uses. This one holds the operating system and the two or three models in active use. The board carries no SATA sockets — its eight SATA ports come out of two Mini-SAS HD connectors, silkscreened SATA_0_3 and SATA_4_7, and need a breakout cable — which is why the archive drive stays external over USB.
    Looks right when

    The stick lies flat and parallel to the board, screwed down at its far end, chips facing up.

    Stop if

    The stick will not go in far enough for the screw hole to reach the standoff. The gold edge is not fully in the slot; take it out and re-insert at the angle. And if the drive is missing in the BIOS at step 8, check first that it is in M2_2.

  5. The cooler

    Ground yourself first: wrist strap on the supply’s shell, or touch a grounded metal object. Again every time you sit back down.

    Follow ARCTIC's printed steps for the SP3 / TR4 socket; they are the same mounting for both. The bracket screws come up from the cooler's mounting frame into the threaded holes that already surround the socket, so there is no backplate to fit. The PH1 Phillips is the tool.

    • Clean the lid first. With the processor seated and the socket closed, wipe its metal lid with the cloth and isopropyl until it is bare, dull metal with no grey residue; a used chip usually has old paste on it. Let it dry for a minute. Nothing drips into the socket because the lid sits above the closed frame.
    • Paste. The processor's metal lid is enormous compared with a desktop chip. Squeeze the whole 0.8g syringe onto it and spread it thin and even across the entire lid with the spreader in the box, or the edge of a card. A pea-sized dot in the middle, the desktop habit, leaves most of this lid dry. The syringe is one application; there is no second try without more paste.
    • Orientation. Each fan has an arrow on its frame showing which way air moves. Mount the tower so the arrows point at the rear panel, the edge with the VGA and network ports. On a bench there is no case to dictate this; the direction still matters because the board was designed for air to leave at that edge, past the finned voltage-regulator heatsinks that sit beside the socket. Memory is on both sides of the socket and gets air either way.
    • Set the heatsink down squarely, start all bracket screws by hand, then tighten in a cross pattern a few turns each until they all stop. Do not fully tighten one corner before the others have started.
    • Clip both fans on. Plug the fan lead into FAN1. This board has no processor fan header — its seven headers are FAN1 to FAN7 and the layout calls every one a System Fan Connector, so there is no "CPU fan" socket to find and no CPU-fan alarm to trip. FAN1 is simply the nearest. If the box had two separate leads and no splitter, the second goes to FAN2.
    PWMthe 4-pin fan connection where the board sets the speed rather than the fan choosing it. Every header here is a system fan header, so speed comes from the board’s fan policy in the BIOS and from the management controller, not from a processor-fan rule. Whichever header you use, confirm at step 9 that it reports a sensible RPM.
    Looks right when

    The tower does not rock when you push its top gently; the arrows point at the ports; the fan lead is in FAN1.

    Stop if

    The heatsink lands on the memory clips or the cooler cannot sit flat. Check you used the SP3 / TR4 hardware and not another socket's.

  6. Power supply cables

    Ground yourself first: wrist strap on the supply’s shell, or touch a grounded metal object. Again every time you sit back down.

    The supply is fully modular: nothing is attached until you attach it. Only three cables run from it to the board in this build. No graphics card means no graphics-card cable, and the board’s own GFX_12V1 auxiliary graphics power connector stays empty too. The AC cord is still out of the wall, per step 0 and the board’s manual; it goes in at step 7. The supply’s switch stays off throughout this step.

    • 24-pin motherboard cable → ATXPWR1. It has a latch; push until it clicks, then tug to confirm.
    • CPU cable #1, the 8-pin end → ATX12V1, the 8-pin socket. Keep its two 4-pin halves together.
    • CPU cable #2, split into 4+4 → one 4-pin half into ATX12V2. The other half hangs free; tie it off. Both 12V connectors get populated. Confirmed from the board’s own sheet, 14 Sep 2026: section 6 draws all three power connectors with their pinouts — a 24-pin, an 8-pin 12V and a 4-pin 12V — so the counts this guide quotes are the board’s, not an inference. What the sheet still does not say in words is whether the 4-pin is mandatory; it shows it as part of power installation, and filling both is correct either way.
    • AC cord: still out of the wall. Check the rocker is on "O". Both go live at step 7.

    Every plug is keyed and latched. If one seems to fit backwards, it is the wrong plug or the wrong socket: the 8-pin CPU plug and the 8-pin graphics-card plug on the supply side look similar but are not interchangeable, and the cable labels say which is which.

    EPS / ATX12Vthe 12-volt cables that feed the processor’s voltage regulators directly, separate from the 24-pin that feeds everything else. A 155W server processor with eight memory channels draws more through them than a desktop chip, hence two connectors — one 8-pin and one 4-pin, twelve pins between them, half carrying 12V and half ground.
    Looks right when

    Three cables into the board, all latched, the spare 4-pin half tied off, the supply resting beside the board with its fan facing open air and nothing lying on the memory.

    Stop if

    A plug needs force or sits proud of its socket. Check the key. Never power on with a half-seated 12V connector.

  7. Monitor, keyboard, network

    • Monitor cable → the board’s VGA1 port on the rear panel (or the adapter, then the monitor). Not COM1 beside it — that is a 9-pin serial port and the plug will not fit anyway.
    • Keyboard → any rear USB port.
    • Ethernet cable #1 → the dedicated management port, silkscreened IPMI_LAN1 and the only network port that is not one of the 10GbE pair; other end into the router.
    • Ethernet cable #2 → LAN1, other end into the router.
    • Nothing else. Not the G-DRIVE, not the memtest stick yet.

    Once the supply is switched on at step 7, the port LEDs tell you what negotiated — which is how you settle the open question about the desktop’s link speed without installing anything. From the board’s sheet, section 8:

    PortSpeed LED offYellowGreen
    IPMI_LAN110 Mbps, or no link100 Mbps1 Gbps
    LAN1 / LAN2100 Mbps or no link — and also 1 Gbps1 Gbps on R3.0X boards only10 Gbps

    The odd one is the 10GbE pair: a dark speed LED with a live link means 1 Gbps, not a fault. Only green means 10 Gbps. On both ports the other LED is activity — off for no link, on for link, blinking yellow for traffic.

    Looks right when

    Two network cables in two different ports, the management one on its own away from the LAN1/LAN2 pair.

    Stop if

    You cannot tell the management port from the two 10GbE ports. The quick guide’s I/O Panel drawing labels all eight items — IPMI_LAN1 is item 5, LAN1 and LAN2 are 6 and 7. Do not guess: the management page will not appear on the wrong port.

  8. First power

    Two things happen in sequence, and the gap between them is where first builds get abandoned.

    • Plug the AC cord into the wall now — this is the first time it goes in — and then switch the supply on (the "I" position). Nothing visible should happen except perhaps a status LED on the board and the fans twitching or spinning for a moment. The management controller is now booting on standby power. Give it two minutes. The screen stays black. This is normal.
    • Press power: the management page’s power button, the screwdriver across the two power-button pins for a second, or the pigtail. The fans start.

    Which two pins — answered, 14 Sep 2026

    The board’s Quick Installation Guide prints the PANEL1 pinout in section 9. It is eight pins in two rows of four, with pin 1 marked at the lower left:

    • Wait. A server processor with 128GB of memory can take two to four minutes on its very first boot before anything appears on screen, because the board is measuring every memory channel. A black screen with fans running is not a failure until five full minutes have passed. Then the ASRock Rack splash appears on the VGA monitor.
    POSTpower-on self-test, the board's own checks before any operating system. The first POST after new memory is installed is slow, and every later one is much faster.

    No splash after five minutes

    Read Dr. Debug first. The two-digit display near TPM1 holds the code for the stage the board stopped at, and it turns a blind hunt into one lookup. Photograph it before you change anything, then find it in the full User’s Manual’s code table. There will be no beeps to listen for: the board has a SPEAKER1 header but no speaker is part of this build, so the display is the whole signal.

    Then switch the supply off and check in this order, each one fully: both 12V connectors seated (the usual cause); the 24-pin latched; all eight memory clips locked; the three socket screws snug in order; the monitor on VGA1 and switched to that input. Switch on, wait two minutes for the management controller, press power, wait five.

  9. First visit to the BIOS

    Tap Del once a second from the moment the splash appears until the setup screen opens (the splash names the key; on ASRock Rack boards it is Del or F2). This is the BIOS, the board's own settings. Read, change almost nothing.

    • Confirm the processor: EPYC 7452, 32 cores.
    • Confirm the memory: 131072 MB (that is 128GB) at 3200 MT/s. If it reads 65536 or 98304 MB, one or more sticks are not seated: power off and check every clip again.
    • Confirm the SSD appears in the storage or NVMe list.
    • Note the management IP. Under Server Mgmt → BMC Network Configuration the board shows the address it got from the router. Write it down. If it shows 0.0.0.0, the management cable is in the wrong port or the router has not handed out an address yet.
    • Set boot mode to UEFI and set the date and time.
    • Leave everything else at defaults for now. The NUMA setting stays at NPS1, SMT stays on.
    UEFIthe modern boot method. The operating-system installer and memtest86+ both expect it; the older "legacy" mode is not needed for anything here.
    Looks right when

    32 cores, 131072 MB, one NVMe drive, a management IP that starts like your other home devices' addresses.

    Stop if

    The memory speed reads 2933 or lower with all eight sticks detected. Look for a memory-speed setting left at "auto" and check the kit is the 3200 part you ordered.

  10. Log in to the management page

    From the desktop, open a browser at the management IP from step 8, over https. The browser will warn that the connection is not private; that is expected, the board signed its own certificate, and the page is on your home network. Choose the advanced or proceed option to continue. Log in with admin / admin. The board makes you change the password immediately. Put the new one in the repo's encrypted secrets file (the sops file the plan describes), not in this document and not in a note on the bench.

    • Sensors: read and write down the idle temperatures for the processor, the voltage regulators (VRM) and the memory (DIMM). The plan's later thermal check (its step A4) reads the same sensors under a full inference load and decides whether the bench needs a fan; without idle values there is nothing to compare.
    • Remote control / console: the machine's screen and keyboard, in the browser tab. From now on the VGA monitor and the keyboard on the bench are optional.
    • Power control: on, off, reset. This is the power button for the rest of the machine's life.
    Looks right when

    The sensors page shows live readings and the console shows the BIOS screen you were just looking at.

    Stop if

    The page does not load. Confirm the cable is in the management port, not LAN1; check the router's list of connected devices for a new one; wait two more minutes and retry.

  11. memtest86+ overnight, inside the return window

    This is the step with a deadline. A faulty stick found now is a defective-product return, paid by the vendor. The same stick found in month four is a warranty claim that needs a matched replacement on a board that wants all eight identical. The window is 30 days from delivery: the parts were confirmed in hand on 13 September 2026, which puts the last day at 13 October 2026 — sooner if the invoice shows the kit was delivered before then. Work to the invoice date.

    • On the desktop, download memtest86+ from memtest.org (the same files are on its GitHub releases page). Take the free, open memtest86+, not the similarly named commercial MemTest86; either works, this one costs nothing. On Windows use the "USB installer" download, which writes the stick itself. On Linux or macOS take the .img and write it with dd to the stick's base device, not a partition. Either way the stick is erased.
    • Plug the stick into a rear USB port on the board. Power on (or reset) and press the boot-menu key the splash names (usually F11 on ASRock boards). Choose the USB stick; pick the entry that starts with "UEFI" if two are offered.
    • Let it run all passes, all night, with all eight sticks installed. The screen counts passes and errors.
    • In the morning: zero errors across at least two completed passes is a pass; with 128GB a single pass takes an hour or more, so a full night gives several. Photograph the screen with the pass count and date visible.
    • Any error at all: power off, leave one stick in DDR4_A1 — the quick guide names the slots but not the single-module one, and A1 is the convention; the full User’s Manual confirms it — and test again; rotate through the eight until the failing stick is found. Return that stick under the vendor’s defective-return process, not a warranty claim.
    Looks right when

    Errors: 0, two or more completed passes, 128GB shown at the top of the memtest screen.

    Stop if

    memtest shows less than 128GB. A stick is not detected; go back to step 8 before testing further.

  12. Record, then hand over to the plan

    Everything from this point is software, and it starts from the plan's step A3, the operating-system install. Before that, put five things in the Studio-G repository's notes/ directory so they are never re-derived:

    • Photos of the finished bench, all connectors visible.
    • The management IP.
    • The idle temperatures from step 9, with the date.
    • The memtest result: passes, errors, date, photo.
    • The Dr. Debug code table, and whether ATX12V2 is required or optional — the last two things the printed sheet does not answer. Both 12V connectors were populated either way.

    The new management password goes in the encrypted secrets file only, never in notes/.
