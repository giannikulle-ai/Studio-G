PW-1x Assembly Guide

Every part is bought. This is the order to put them together, what each step should look and feel like, and where to stop and check. Written for a first server build: nothing is assumed, every term is explained the first time it appears, and each risky step has a drawing. The graphics card stays in the desktop; PW-1x runs on its processor alone until a card is bought.

Tick each row as you confirm it. The first table is what the Build Sheet says was bought or owned. Part 2 · The build

Twelve steps, in this order

Each step says what you are doing, what it should look or feel like when it is right, and the one condition where you stop instead of pushing on. Steps 1 to 5 are the board on the bench with no cable between it and the power supply. Step 7 is the first time the supply's switch is turned on. Budget an unhurried two to three hours for steps 0 to 9, then the night for step 10.

  1. Before opening anything

    Open bench means the board lies flat on its box with the parts mounted on it and the power supply beside it, no case. Workshop servers run like this for years. What it costs you is that nothing pushes air across the board except the cooler's own fans; step 9 reads the temperatures that tell you whether that matters.

    Static. Plug the power supply's AC cord into the supply and the wall now, with the supply's rocker switch off (the "O" position), and leave it there. Its steel shell is now grounded: touch it before you pick up any part, and again whenever you have walked away and come back. If you bought the wrist strap, clip it to that shell and wear it for the whole build; then forgetting is impossible. Nothing else is connected to the supply yet, so nothing can be energised. Leave every part in its bag until the step that uses it.

    Find these on the real board with the manual's layout page open (it is near the front of the printed manual). You will need every one of them:

    • The processor socket: a large square with a hinged metal frame and three screws.
    • The eight memory slots, four on each side of the socket, labelled A1 through H1.
    • The first M.2 slot, a short horizontal connector with a screw post along its length.
    • ATXPWR1, the wide 24-pin power connector along one edge.
    • ATX12V1, an 8-pin power connector, and ATX12V2, a 4-pin one. Both feed the processor. Both get plugged in.
    • The front-panel header: a block of small pins that a case's power button would plug into. Note which two are the power button.
    • The processor fan header, the 4-pin or 6-pin header nearest the socket. ASRock Rack labels it CPU_FAN1 on most boards; the layout page confirms the name here.
    • On the rear panel: the VGA port, the USB ports, two 10GbE network ports labelled LAN1 and LAN2, and a third, separate network port for management, set apart from the pair and labelled for it (the manual's rear-panel drawing names it).
    IPMI / BMCthe board has a small computer of its own, the baseboard management controller, that runs whenever the power supply is switched on, even with the machine "off". It serves a web page on the management port where you can power the machine on, watch the screen and read every temperature sensor. It is also what drives the VGA port.
    Looks right when

    You can point to all eight items above on the board without the manual.

    Stop if

    Any label in the manual does not match what is printed on the board. Boards have revisions; trust the print on the board.

  2. Processor into the socket

    Ground yourself first: touch the supply's bare shell, or clip the wrist strap to it. Again every time you sit back down.

    This is the one step where force in the wrong place does permanent damage, and it is also simple if you follow the order. The socket's metal frame is held by three T20 Torx screws, numbered 1, 2 and 3; the numbers are stamped into the frame beside each screw. Open them 3, 2, 1. Close them 1, 2, 3. The screws are captive: they loosen but do not come out.

    • Loosen 3, then 2, then 1. Lift the outer force frame up on its hinge.
    • Under it is a thinner rail frame. Lift it up on its hinge too. Now the socket's gold contacts are exposed. Do not touch them and do not let anything fall on them.
    • Take the processor, still in its plastic carrier. There is a small triangle on one corner of the carrier and a matching triangle on the rail frame. Match them.
    • Slide the carrier into the rail frame's guides from the top until it clicks. It should sit flat in the rail frame with no effort; you are sliding plastic into plastic, not pushing the processor into anything.
    • Lower the rail frame. Lower the force frame over it.
    • Tighten 1, then 2, then 3. Each screw turns freely, then stops. That stop is the end of travel: the socket is designed so that seated is seated. If you have a torque driver, 1.58 N·m; if not, snug and stop.
    carrier framethe plastic surround the processor ships in. On this socket the processor is never handled bare; the carrier is what you hold, and it stays on the chip inside the socket.
    Feels right when

    The carrier slid in with no resistance and clicked; all three screws turned freely and stopped at the same firmness.

    Stop if

    A screw resists before it stops, or the frame will not sit flat. Open 3-2-1 and check the carrier is fully in its guides. Never push harder.

  3. Memory, all eight sticks

    Ground yourself first: touch the supply's bare shell, or clip the wrist strap to it. Again every time you sit back down.

    Every slot gets a stick, so there is no choice about which slots to use. The board is one DIMM per channel: eight channels, eight slots, and the bandwidth this whole machine is built around only exists with all eight filled.

    • Open the latch at each end of the slot by pushing it outward (some slots have a fixed end and one latch; then open the one).
    • Look at the gold edge of the stick: the notch is off-centre. Turn the stick until the notch lines up with the ridge in the slot. It only fits one way.
    • Set the stick in the slot, then press straight down on both ends at once, evenly. The latch swings up and clicks closed on its own when the stick is seated. That click is the confirmation.
    • Repeat for all eight. Then run a finger along every latch: each one closed, every stick at the same height.
    ECC RDIMMserver memory. ECC means it detects and corrects single-bit errors instead of silently corrupting data; registered (the R) means a buffer chip on each stick lets the board run eight of them at full speed. It is not interchangeable with desktop memory.
    Feels right when

    Each stick took firm, even pressure and its latch closed by itself.

    Stop if

    A stick needs real force. It is turned the wrong way. Take it out and match the notch.

  4. The SSD into the first M.2 slot

    Ground yourself first: touch the supply's bare shell, or clip the wrist strap to it. Again every time you sit back down.

    The Kingston stick is 80mm long, size 2280. The board's slot has screw holes at several lengths; the standoff (a small threaded post) goes in the hole at the 80mm mark, from the bag that came with the board.

    • Screw the standoff into the 80mm hole finger-tight.
    • Hold the stick at about 20 degrees and push its gold edge into the slot until it stops. It will spring up at an angle; that is normal.
    • Press it flat against the standoff and fix it with the small screw. Snug, not tight: the screw only stops the stick springing back up.
    M.2 / NVMea solid-state drive that plugs straight into the board and talks over PCI Express, the same bus a graphics card uses. This one holds the operating system and the two or three models in active use. The board has no SATA ports at all, which is why the archive drive stays external over USB.
    Looks right when

    The stick lies flat and parallel to the board, screwed down at its far end, chips facing up.

    Stop if

    The stick will not go in far enough for the screw hole to reach the standoff. The gold edge is not fully in the slot; take it out and re-insert at the angle.

  5. The cooler

    Ground yourself first: touch the supply's bare shell, or clip the wrist strap to it. Again every time you sit back down.

    Follow ARCTIC's printed steps for the SP3 / TR4 socket; they are the same mounting for both. The bracket screws come up from the cooler's mounting frame into the threaded holes that already surround the socket, so there is no backplate to fit. The PH1 Phillips is the tool.

    • Clean the lid first. With the processor seated and the socket closed, wipe its metal lid with the cloth and isopropyl until it is bare, dull metal with no grey residue; a used chip usually has old paste on it. Let it dry for a minute. Nothing drips into the socket because the lid sits above the closed frame.
    • Paste. The processor's metal lid is enormous compared with a desktop chip. Squeeze the whole 0.8g syringe onto it and spread it thin and even across the entire lid with the spreader in the box, or the edge of a card. A pea-sized dot in the middle, the desktop habit, leaves most of this lid dry. The syringe is one application; there is no second try without more paste.
    • Orientation. Each fan has an arrow on its frame showing which way air moves. Mount the tower so the arrows point at the rear panel, the edge with the VGA and network ports. On a bench there is no case to dictate this; the direction still matters because the board was designed for air to leave at that edge, past the finned voltage-regulator heatsinks that sit beside the socket. Memory is on both sides of the socket and gets air either way.
    • Set the heatsink down squarely, start all bracket screws by hand, then tighten in a cross pattern a few turns each until they all stop. Do not fully tighten one corner before the others have started.
    • Clip both fans on. Plug the fan lead into CPU_FAN1. If the box had two separate leads and no splitter, the second goes to the next fan header along; the manual's layout page names them.
    PWMthe 4-pin fan connection where the board sets the speed. With the lead in CPU_FAN1 the board ramps the fans with processor temperature; in any other header it may run them flat out or complain about a missing CPU fan at boot.
    Looks right when

    The tower does not rock when you push its top gently; the arrows point at the ports; the fan lead is in CPU_FAN1.

    Stop if

    The heatsink lands on the memory latches or the cooler cannot sit flat. Check you used the SP3 / TR4 hardware and not another socket's.

  6. Power supply cables

    Ground yourself first: touch the supply's bare shell, or clip the wrist strap to it. Again every time you sit back down.

    The supply is fully modular: nothing is attached until you attach it. Only three cables run from it to the board in this build, plus the AC cord that has been in since step 0. No graphics card means no graphics-card cable. The supply's switch stays off throughout this step.

    • 24-pin motherboard cable → ATXPWR1. It has a latch; push until it clicks, then tug to confirm.
    • CPU cable #1, the 8-pin end → ATX12V1, the 8-pin socket. Keep its two 4-pin halves together.
    • CPU cable #2, split into 4+4 → one 4-pin half into ATX12V2, the 4-pin socket. The other half hangs free; tie it off. Both 12V connectors get populated. The board has both — ATX12V1 8-pin and ATX12V2 4-pin, from ASRock's published connector list — but whether the 4-pin is strictly required is not something this guide could confirm, so check the manual's power section. Filling both is correct either way.
    • AC cord: already in from step 0. Check the rocker is still on "O".

    Every plug is keyed and latched. If one seems to fit backwards, it is the wrong plug or the wrong socket: the 8-pin CPU plug and the 8-pin graphics-card plug on the supply side look similar but are not interchangeable, and the cable labels say which is which.

    EPS / ATX12Vthe 12-volt cables that feed the processor's voltage regulators directly, separate from the 24-pin that feeds everything else. A 155W server processor with eight memory channels draws more through them than a desktop chip, hence two connectors.
    Looks right when

    Three cables into the board, all latched, the spare 4-pin half tied off, the supply resting beside the board with its fan facing open air and nothing lying on the memory.

    Stop if

    A plug needs force or sits proud of its socket. Check the key. Never power on with a half-seated 12V connector.

  7. Monitor, keyboard, network

    • Monitor cable → the board's VGA port on the rear panel (or the adapter, then the monitor).
    • Keyboard → any rear USB port.
    • Ethernet cable #1 → the dedicated management port (IPMI / MGMT), other end into the router.
    • Ethernet cable #2 → LAN1, other end into the router.
    • Nothing else. Not the G-DRIVE, not the memtest stick yet.
    Looks right when

    Two network cables in two different ports, the management one on its own away from the LAN1/LAN2 pair.

    Stop if

    You cannot tell the management port from the two 10GbE ports. The manual's rear-panel drawing labels them; do not guess, the management page will not appear on the wrong port.

  8. First power

    Two things happen in sequence, and the gap between them is where first builds get abandoned.

    • Switch the supply on (the "I" position). Nothing visible should happen except perhaps a status LED on the board and the fans twitching or spinning for a moment. The management controller is now booting on standby power. Give it two minutes. The screen stays black. This is normal.
    • Press power: the management page's power button, the screwdriver across the two power-button pins for a second, or the pigtail. The fans start.
    • Wait. A server processor with 128GB of memory can take two to four minutes on its very first boot before anything appears on screen, because the board is measuring every memory channel. A black screen with fans running is not a failure until five full minutes have passed. Then the ASRock Rack splash appears on the VGA monitor.
    POSTpower-on self-test, the board's own checks before any operating system. The first POST after new memory is installed is slow, and every later one is much faster.

    No splash after five minutes

    Switch the supply off, then check in this order, each one fully: both 12V connectors seated (the usual cause); the 24-pin latched; all eight memory latches closed; the three socket screws snug in 1-2-3 order; the monitor on the board's VGA port and switched to that input. Switch on, wait two minutes for the management controller, press power, wait five. If it still shows nothing, the board's status LEDs or beep pattern are the next clue; the manual lists what they mean.

  9. First visit to the BIOS

    Tap Del once a second from the moment the splash appears until the setup screen opens (the splash names the key; on ASRock Rack boards it is Del or F2). This is the BIOS, the board's own settings. Read, change almost nothing.

    • Confirm the processor: EPYC 7452, 32 cores.
    • Confirm the memory: 131072 MB (that is 128GB) at 3200 MT/s. If it reads 65536 or 98304 MB, one or more sticks are not seated: power off and check the latches again.
    • Confirm the SSD appears in the storage or NVMe list.
    • Note the management IP. Under Server Mgmt → BMC Network Configuration the board shows the address it got from the router. Write it down. If it shows 0.0.0.0, the management cable is in the wrong port or the router has not handed out an address yet.
    • Set boot mode to UEFI and set the date and time.
    • Leave everything else at defaults for now. The NUMA setting stays at NPS1, SMT stays on.
    UEFIthe modern boot method. The operating-system installer and memtest86+ both expect it; the older "legacy" mode is not needed for anything here.
    Looks right when

    32 cores, 131072 MB, one NVMe drive, a management IP that starts like your other home devices' addresses.

    Stop if

    The memory speed reads 2933 or lower with all eight sticks detected. Look for a memory-speed setting left at "auto" and check the kit is the 3200 part you ordered.

  10. Log in to the management page

    From the desktop, open a browser at the management IP from step 8, over https. The browser will warn that the connection is not private; that is expected, the board signed its own certificate, and the page is on your home network. Choose the advanced or proceed option to continue. Log in with admin / admin. The board makes you change the password immediately. Put the new one in the repo's encrypted secrets file (the sops file the plan describes), not in this document and not in a note on the bench.

    • Sensors: read and write down the idle temperatures for the processor, the voltage regulators (VRM) and the memory (DIMM). The plan's later thermal check (its step A4) reads the same sensors under a full inference load and decides whether the bench needs a fan; without idle values there is nothing to compare.
    • Remote control / console: the machine's screen and keyboard, in the browser tab. From now on the VGA monitor and the keyboard on the bench are optional.
    • Power control: on, off, reset. This is the power button for the rest of the machine's life.
    Looks right when

    The sensors page shows live readings and the console shows the BIOS screen you were just looking at.

    Stop if

    The page does not load. Confirm the cable is in the management port, not LAN1; check the router's list of connected devices for a new one; wait two more minutes and retry.

  11. memtest86+ overnight, inside the return window

    This is the step with a deadline. A faulty stick found now is a defective-product return, paid by the vendor. The same stick found in month four is a warranty claim that needs a matched replacement on a board that wants all eight identical. The window is 30 days from delivery.

    • On the desktop, download memtest86+ from memtest.org (the same files are on its GitHub releases page). Take the free, open memtest86+, not the similarly named commercial MemTest86; either works, this one costs nothing. On Windows use the "USB installer" download, which writes the stick itself. On Linux or macOS take the .img and write it with dd to the stick's base device, not a partition. Either way the stick is erased.
    • Plug the stick into a rear USB port on the board. Power on (or reset) and press the boot-menu key the splash names (usually F11 on ASRock boards). Choose the USB stick; pick the entry that starts with "UEFI" if two are offered.
    • Let it run all passes, all night, with all eight sticks installed. The screen counts passes and errors.
    • In the morning: zero errors across at least two completed passes is a pass; with 128GB a single pass takes an hour or more, so a full night gives several. Photograph the screen with the pass count and date visible.
    • Any error at all: power off, leave one stick in the slot the manual names for a single module (on most boards A1), test again; rotate through the eight until the failing stick is found. Return that stick under the vendor's defective-return process, not a warranty claim.
    Looks right when

    Errors: 0, two or more completed passes, 128GB shown at the top of the memtest screen.

    Stop if

    memtest shows less than 128GB. A stick is not detected; go back to step 8 before testing further.

  12. Record, then hand over to the plan

    Everything from this point is software, and it starts from the plan's step A3, the operating-system install. Before that, put five things in the Studio-G repository's notes/ directory so they are never re-derived:

    • Photos of the finished bench, all connectors visible.
    • The management IP.
    • The idle temperatures from step 9, with the date.
    • The memtest result: passes, errors, date, photo.
    • The answer to the Build Sheet's open question, read off the manual at assembly: whether ATX12V2 is required or optional. Both were populated either way.

    The new management password goes in the encrypted secrets file only, never in notes/.
